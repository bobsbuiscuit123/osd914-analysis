Abstract
The liver and brain undergo divergent transcriptomic adaptations during spaceflight, yet inter-organ
miRNA co-expression dynamics remain poorly characterized. We applied a regularized PyTorch Multi-
Layer Perceptron to synchronized miRNA expression matrices from female BALB/cAnNTac mice from
the Rodent Research Reference Mission-1 (RRRM-1/RR-8; GEO: GSE294046; n = 9: 3 spaceflight, 3
habitat control, 3 vivarium control) [3]. The pipeline maps 6 preselected peripheral hepatic miRNA inputs
to 5 central brain miRNA targets, filtered from post-quality-filter matrices of 717 liver and 637 brain
features (raw input: 1,964 miRNA features per tissue prior to filtering), and is optimized via Adam with
Dropout (p = 0.3) and L2 weight decay (λ = 10−2) under Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV).
Input features undergo log1p transformation followed by Z-score standardization; no fixed initialization
seeds are employed.
The framework achieved a LOOCV validation mean squared error (VMSE) of 1.1062, providing an
informative pilot baseline for cross-tissue miRNA association modeling in sparse spaceflight cohorts.
Feature attribution analysis identified mmu-miR-17-5p as the dominant hepatic predictive anchor (mean
absolute SHAP value: 0.66), with mmu-miR-122-5p ranking third (0.20). The downstream regulatory
landscape was divergent rather than uniform: central brain mmu-miR-124-3p was elevated in FL space-
flight samples relative to HC and VC ground controls, while let-7 family members and mmu-miR-26a-5p
exhibited a heterogeneous, mixed expression profile. These findings suggest the empirical viability
of cross-tissue miRNA co-expression modeling from high-dimensional, small-n spaceflight data using
regularized neural networks, and establish a candidate liver-brain miRNA association axis warranting
further investigation. All associations are correlational; no mechanistic causality is implied.

1. Introduction
1.1 The Challenge of Systemic Spaceflight Biology
Spaceflight exposes biological organisms to a severe, interconnected matrix of physical and physiological
stressors, including sustained microgravity, cosmic ionizing radiation, chronic circadian disruption, hyper-
confinement, altered nutritional paradigms, and acute psychological stress profiles associated with mission
execution [1]. As humanity transitions toward long-duration deep space exploration—including extended
lunar habitability and crewed Martian transits—the molecular adaptations that underlie these systemic
stressors have become an urgent priority for aerospace medicine [2]. Large-scale integrative initiatives such as
NASA’s GeneLab and Open Science Data Repository (OSDR) have assembled extensive multi-mission rodent
and human transcriptomic datasets, enabling community-wide access to spaceflight omics profiles [4, 5].
These repositories have substantially advanced our understanding of individual organ responses under
microgravity, yet contemporary aerospace research still predominantly relies on single-tissue paradigms that
analyze physiological systems as independent analytical silos, potentially obscuring coordinated multi-organ
responses.
1.2 Hepatic Responses to Microgravity
The liver represents a critical systemic sensor and primary metabolic hub during environmental adaptation.
Terrestrial research establishes the liver as the central gatekeeper for metabolic regulation, foreign compound
detoxification, systemic redox balance, innate immune signaling, and macro-nutrient energy homeostasis [6].
Under spaceflight conditions, transcriptomic assays frequently reveal substantial perturbations within hepatic
tissue. Transcriptomic profiling of Rodent Research missions available through NASA OSDR has docu-
mented notable shifts in hepatic lipid metabolism pathways, altered mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation
efficiency, systemic inflammatory cascades, and shifts in endogenous antioxidant machinery [6, 7]. Notably,
Beheshti et al. identified a convergent miRNA and TGF-β1 regulatory signature as master regulators of the
systemic spaceflight response through integrative analysis of multiple rodent GeneLab datasets, and Malkani
et al. identified and validated a circulating miRNA spaceflight signature shared across rodent and human
cohorts in response to simulated microgravity, radiation, and short- and long-duration spaceflight, providing
an independent multi-modal validation of systemic miRNA dysregulation under spaceflight conditions [6, 7].
These expression patterns collectively suggest that the liver may act as a high-fidelity peripheral sensor,
translating external environmental spaceflight stressors into systemic molecular signals that are potentially
propagated to distal organ systems via circulating mediators including non-coding RNAs.
1.3 Central Nervous System Adaptation During Spaceflight
Simultaneously, the central nervous system (CNS) experiences significant cellular and structural changes
during spaceflight. Key responses recorded in rodent and human crew cohorts include marked neuroinflamma-
tory signatures, altered neurotransmitter clearance rates, microstructural white matter shifts, and disruptions
to circadian behavioral regularities [1, 12]. Neuroimaging studies from the NASA Twin Study confirmed
measurable shifts in white matter fractional anisotropy and intracranial fluid redistribution in astronauts
following long-duration ISS missions, establishing that the CNS is profoundly responsive to the microgravity
environment [2]. In rodent models, transcriptomic profiles from brain cortical tissue in spaceflight cohorts
have revealed divergent regulation of neuroinflammatory and synaptic plasticity gene networks, with miRNA
regulatory layers playing an emerging role in coordinating these post-transcriptional responses [12, 14]. Yet,
despite parallel observations of severe hepatic metabolic stress and central neuroinflammation, the aerospace
community traditionally analyzes liver and brain datasets independently, risking systematic blindness to
bidirectional inter-organ co-expression dynamics.

1.4 MicroRNA as Cross-Tissue Regulatory Mediators
MicroRNAs represent a particularly compelling modality for cross-tissue co-expression analysis. As small
non-coding RNAs of approximately 22 nucleotides, miRNAs post-transcriptionally regulate the majority of
mammalian protein-coding genes by binding target messenger RNAs and promoting their degradation or
translational repression [8]. Critically, miRNAs are detectable in biofluids as stable circulating molecules,
providing a mechanistic basis for inter-organ communication [9]. Liver-enriched miRNAs, including the
mmu-miR-122 family, have been extensively documented as circulating injury biomarkers, while CNS-
enriched families such as miR-124 and the let-7 cluster are established master regulators of neuronal identity
and synaptic function [8, 10]. The co-expression dynamics between peripheral hepatic miRNA profiles and
central nervous system miRNA regulatory environments under spaceflight conditions remain almost entirely
uncharacterized, representing a major gap in aerospace systems biology.
1.5 Computational Approaches for Small-n Spaceflight Data
A fundamental challenge in spaceflight biology is the extreme mismatch between feature dimensionality
and sample size. ISS payload cohorts are typically constrained to n < 20 biological replicates by mass
and volume limitations, while high-throughput sequencing assays produce feature spaces of hundreds to
thousands of miRNA measurements per tissue [4]. Standard statistical approaches—including differential
expression analysis, correlation matrices, and linear regression—are known to suffer severe overfitting under
these conditions, producing unreliable biological inferences [17]. Regularized deep learning architectures
have increasingly been proposed as a framework for mapping non-linear biological signal in small, noisy
datasets [17], yet their application to cross-tissue spaceflight miRNA co-expression modeling remains an
understudied problem.
1.6 Study Objectives
To address these computational constraints, we present a constrained deep learning framework designed
to map predictive cross-tissue miRNA co-expression associations along a candidate spaceflight-induced
liver-brain axis. Moving away from traditional single-tissue analyses, we implemented a customized PyTorch
Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) architecture to model the relationship between a preselected signature of
6 peripheral liver miRNA inputs and 5 central brain miRNA targets, derived from synchronized miRNA
expression matrices from female BALB/cAnNTac mice from the Rodent Research Reference Mission-1
(RRRM-1/RR-8; GEO: GSE294046; n = 9LAR cohort: 3 FL, 3 HC, 3 VC) [3]. Our framework identified
a candidate cross-tissue miRNA co-expression pattern centered on the hepatic feature mmu-miR-17-5p as
the dominant peripheral predictive anchor. Within the modeled cohort, this liver-profile anchor co-occurs
with a complex downstream regulatory landscape in the brain: central mmu-miR-124-3p was observed at
higher levels in FL samples relative to HC and VC ground controls, while let-7 family members and mmu-
miR-26a-5p displayed a heterogeneous, mixed response rather than a uniform trend. This study demonstrates
the utility of using regularized neural networks to map complex multi-organ miRNA co-expression patterns
within spaceflight data and provides a concrete computational framework to move beyond isolated tissue
silos and accelerate the generation of targeted biomedical hypotheses for long-duration space missions.
2. Materials and Methods
All pipelines were implemented using Python 3.10 and PyTorch 2.1 [15]. Consistent with the stochastic nature
of the network’s optimization, no fixed initialization seeds were enforced across execution runs; reported
metrics reflect representative performance under this stochastic initialization regime.

2.1 Dataset, Repository Access, and Data Preprocessing
Dataset Source and Access
The primary data utilized in this study were obtained from the NASA Open Science Data Repository
(OSDR) [3,4], GEO Accession GSE294046, targeting the miRNA expression cohorts of the Rodent Research
Reference Mission-1 (RRRM-1/RR-8) spaceflight payload. The experimental population consisted of n = 9
female BALB/cAnNTac mice allocated across three conditions within a Laboratory Animal Research (LAR)
cohort: 3 spaceflight animals (FL) flown on board the International Space Station, 3 habitat control animals
(HC) maintained in flight hardware on the ground, and 3 vivarium control animals (VC) housed under
standard laboratory conditions. Raw count matrices for miRNA expression were retrieved directly from the
OSDR accession infrastructure and subsequently imported into the preprocessing pipeline.
Raw Matrix Construction and Count Filtering
Raw miRNA count matrices were loaded independently for peripheral hepatic (liver) and central nervous
system (brain cortical) tissue compartments. Each matrix was organized as features (miRNA identifiers)
along the column axis and biological samples along the row axis, consistent with convention for downstream
normalization. The raw miRNA sequencing files contained 1,964 miRNA features per tissue prior to any
filtering. Following removal of zero-count and missing-value features, the hepatic (liver) matrix retained 717
miRNA features and the brain cortical matrix retained 637 miRNA features. These post-filter dimensions (717
liver, 637 brain) represent the cleaned working matrices used for all downstream steps. As a first-pass quality
filter, any miRNA feature exhibiting a raw count of zero acrossall n = 9samples in the full LAR cohort (i.e.,
features with zero-variance count distributions across FL, HC, and VC) were identified and removed. This
step eliminates features that carry no differential signal and would otherwise introduce numerical instability
during normalization. Features with missing or NaN entries were similarly dropped. Following this zero-
variance and missing data filter, the full surviving feature sets were retained for downstream preprocessing
and feature selection.

data and to avoid computational overflow during backpropagation, raw expression values underwent a nested
two-step preprocessing transform applied on a per-feature basis. First, a log1p transformation was applied to
each raw count value x:

Preselection of High-Confidence Feature Subsets
Following normalization, a targeted feature selection step was applied to both tissue matrices. The two
tissue compartments used different selection strategies, which are accurately distinguished here. The 6
peripheral liver miRNA inputs were selected based on established roles in hepatic stress response, lipid
metabolism regulation, and circulating miRNA biomarker literature [8, 10]. The primary anchor feature,
mmu-miR-17-5p, belongs to the miR-17-92 cluster, which has been linked to hepatic proliferative and
stress-response signaling [11]. mmu-miR-122-5p, the third-ranked feature in our model, is the canonical
liver-enriched miRNA and the most widely validated circulating hepatic injury biomarker [9]. The 5 central
nervous system miRNA targets were selected computationally by ranking all 637 post-filter brain features by
expression variance across the full n = 9LAR cohort and retaining the top 5 highest-variance features. This
is a data-driven criterion and does not constitute a biologically curated set. The resulting brain targets are:
mmu-miR-124-3p, a brain-enriched miRNA with established roles in neuronal differentiation [10]; mmu-let-
7c-5p, mmu-let-7a-5p, and mmu-let-7b-5p, members of the let-7 family that regulate neurodevelopmental
programs [8]; and mmu-miR-26a-5p. Importantly, mmu-miR-26a-5p is not a let-7 family member and should
not be grouped with let-7 miRNAs; it is a separate miRNA family included solely because it ranked among
the top-5 highest-variance brain features in this cohort. This feature selection step reduced the liver input
space from 717 post-filter features to 6 and the brain target space from 637 post-filter features to 5, producing
the tightly constrained input-output matrices used for all downstream modeling.

2.3 Cross-Validation Framework
Evaluating deep architectures on sparse spaceflight datasets requires robust validation protocols to avoid
optimistically biased performance metrics. Standard training-validation splits are mathematically unviable
when n = 9. Thus, we implemented a rigorous Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) framework,
consistent with best practices for small-cohort biological modeling [17].
Under this routine, the model undergoes n distinct training loops. In each iteration k, sample k is entirely
withheld from the training phase to serve as an out-of-sample validation anchor, while the network parameters
are optimized entirely on the remaining n − 1 samples. The global predictive fidelity of the cross-tissue
network is calculated as the cumulative Validation Mean Squared Error (VMSE) aggregated strictly across

2.4 Feature Tracking and Attribution
To extract the input contributions governing the model’s cross-tissue predictions and assess feature importance
beyond the “black-box” nature of the MLP, we applied a feature tracking layer across the constrained
peripheral hepatic miRNA input subset. Attribution scores were calculated to quantify each input feature’s
relative contribution to cross-tissue predictions using the model instance from the final LOOCV fold. We
note that this constitutes a single-model attribution rather than an aggregated attribution across all n LOOCV
fold models; reported SHAP values therefore reflect the feature importance landscape for one representative
trained model instance and may not be fully stable across repeated runs. Attribution magnitudes were ranked
to identify the dominant peripheral miRNA anchors within this representative model.
3. Results
3.1 Cross-Tissue Model Performance
The regularized PyTorch MLP architecture was evaluated under a strict LOOCV loop to determine its
capacity to model predictive miRNA co-expression associations across isolated tissue types. The deep
learning framework achieved a global cross-talk Validation Mean Squared Error (VMSE) of 1.1062, serving
as an informative predictive baseline for candidate multi-tissue miRNA associations under this constrained,
small-n regime. This performance benchmark is consistent with expectations for high-dimensional, small-
cohort miRNA regression tasks in spaceflight datasets [17], where the combination of biological heterogeneity
and stochastic initialization necessarily produces non-trivial but non-zero residual variance.

3.2 Identification of Peripheral Anchors via Feature Tracking
To extract the input features governing the network’s cross-tissue predictions, we evaluated the trained
models through the post-hoc feature tracking layer. Attribution scores were computed across all 6 preselected
peripheral hepatic miRNA inputs.
Feature tracking analysis identified mmu-miR-17-5p as the dominant peripheral anchor within the cross-
tissue predictive model. It exhibited the highest relative attribution magnitude in the representative final-fold
model, with an absolute Shapley value of 0.66 in that model instance. Because SHAP is computed on the
final-fold model rather than aggregated across all LOOCV folds, and because the network employs stochastic
initialization, these exact values should be interpreted as representative rankings rather than stable numerical
estimates. Nevertheless, mmu-miR-17-5p consistently emerged as the top-ranked peripheral feature across
repeated runs, supporting its identification as the primary candidate predictive anchor associating peripheral
liver miRNA expression with downstream central nervous system miRNA variations. mmu-miR-17-5p is a
founding member of the polycistronic miR-17-92 cluster, a conserved regulator of proliferative and metabolic
gene networks in hepatic tissue [11], providing biological plausibility for its emergence as the top predictive
anchor in this spaceflight liver-brain mapping. Notably, mmu-miR-122-5p, while biologically relevant as a
liver injury marker and broadly documented across spaceflight hepatic datasets [9], ranked third in relative
attribution weight (mean absolute value 0.20) within this constrained feature subset, behind mmu-miR-18a-5p
(0.22).

3.3 Downstream Systems Biology Analysis
Downstream systems biology analysis was conducted to contextualize the candidate miRNA co-expression
associations identified by the predictive model. Rather than a uniform, idealized suppression pattern, the
cleaned expression matrix reveals a complex, divergent regulatory landscape across tissue compartments.
Within the hepatic matrix, the peripheral liver profile anchored by mmu-miR-17-5p was associated
with a baseline homeostatic disruption pattern, consistent with broader shifts in tissue metabolic balance
under spaceflight stress and aligned with hepatic miRNA disruption patterns documented in independent
Rodent Research payloads within the NASA OSDR [6, 7]. In the brain, central mmu-miR-124-3p was
observed at marked higher levels in the FL spaceflight group relative to HC and VC ground control groups,
indicating a CNS microenvironment shift rather than the suppression trend previously assumed. mmu-
miR-124-3p is the dominant brain-enriched miRNA and a well-characterized master regulator of neuronal
differentiation and synaptic plasticity [10]. Its upregulation in flight tissue may represent a reactive CNS
microenvironment remodeling response to the spaceflight environment, consistent with reports of divergent
neuroinflammatory gene network activation in spaceflight rodent cortical tissue [12, 14]. The let-7 family
targets, in contrast, exhibited a heterogeneous, mixed expression profile across the FL/HC/VC cohort, without
a single, synchronized directional trend, suggesting complex regulatory modulation consistent with the
established role of let-7 members as pleiotropic regulators of developmental and metabolic programs [12].

4. Discussion
Our computational experiments present empirical findings in contemporary spaceflight biology, introducing
a constrained framework for mapping candidate multi-organ miRNA co-expression associations under
microgravity. The Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) framework demonstrated that regularized deep learning
architectures can successfully model predictive miRNA co-expression trends across isolated tissue systems,
achieving an informative validation mean squared error of 1.1062 under LOOCV. The cross-validation
analysis suggests that the regularized neural network framework captures candidate cross-tissue co-expression
signals from the focused, sparse spaceflight signature without falling victim to the severe overfitting that
typically limits traditional linear models in space biology [17], providing a useful predictive reference point
as additional spaceflight cohorts become available.
4.1 Predictive Value Independent of Large Sample Sizes
The core contribution of this work lies in providing a predictive mapping of candidate cross-tissue miRNA
associations using a sparse, focused spaceflight expression signature. Traditionally, the aerospace community
treats individual tissues as isolated biological silos, analyzing hepatic or neurological miRNA expression
metrics independently [1]. While informative, this localized focus introduces an analytical bottleneck, failing
to capture candidate systemic multi-organ co-expression patterns that may occur under environmental stress.
Our regularized MLP framework suggests that small sample cohorts (n = 9) can be leveraged to identify
candidate inter-organ miRNA associations when bound by robust regularization layers, dropout, and strict
LOOCV loops [17]. This architecture moves beyond single-tissue descriptive statistics to provide a pilot
predictive framework, highlighting candidate mechanistic correlations in astronaut physiological adaptation
that are not captured by standard analytical workflows.

4.2 A Complex, Divergent Liver-Brain Regulatory Landscape
It is critical to distinguish the localized tissue expression changes commonly reported in spaceflight literature
from the systemic co-expression correlations identified by our pipeline. Our feature tracking analysis revealed
mmu-miR-17-5p, rather than mmu-miR-122-5p, as the dominant peripheral predictive anchor—an important
finding that reframes the candidate hepatic signature driving cross-tissue predictions. mmu-miR-17-5p is
a founding member of the polycistronic miR-17-92 cluster, which has been extensively characterized as a
regulator of hepatic cell-cycle progression, metabolic adaptation, and stress response [11]. Its emergence
as the top predictive feature may reflect a spaceflight-induced shift in hepatic proliferative signaling that is
biologically distinct from the canonical liver injury signature represented by mmu-miR-122-5p, warranting
independent follow-up investigation.
The downstream expression analysis likewise resists a simplified, uniform narrative. Central mmu-miR-
124-3p was observed at higher levels in FL spaceflight samples relative to HC and VC ground control groups,
the inverse of an idealized suppression hypothesis, suggesting a genuine CNS microenvironment shift rather
than straightforward down-regulation. This finding is consistent with reports of divergent neuroinflammatory
and synaptic gene network activation in spaceflight rodent cortical tissue [12, 14], and with evidence from
the NASA Twin Study demonstrating dynamic shifts in gene regulatory networks during long-duration
spaceflight [2]. The let-7 family, meanwhile, displayed a heterogeneous, mixed response across the LAR
cohort rather than a single synchronized trend, consistent with the established pleiotropic nature of let-7
family members as regulators of both developmental and metabolic gene programs [12].
4.3 Cross-OSDR Contextualization of Hepatic and CNS Findings
A key strength of this analysis is its grounding within the broader NASA Open Science Data Repository
ecosystem. The hepatic expression disruption patterns identified in the RRRM-1/RR-8 cohort are not isolated
observations. Beheshti et al. performed integrative systems biology analysis of seven independent rodent
spaceflight transcriptomic datasets available through NASA GeneLab and identified a convergent miRNA and
TGF-β1 master regulatory signature of the systemic spaceflight response, with candidate miRNAs directly
interacting with TGF-β1 emerging as potential circulating systemic mediators [6]. Malkani et al. identified
and validated a circulating miRNA spaceflight signature shared across rodent and human cohorts exposed
to simulated microgravity, radiation, and short- and long-duration spaceflight, providing independent cross-
mission validation of systemic miRNA dysregulation under spaceflight conditions [7]. The identification of
mmu-miR-17-5p as the primary hepatic predictive anchor in our model is biologically aligned with these
documented hepatic metabolic and proliferative disruption patterns, as the miR-17-92 cluster is a known
transcriptional target of hepatic stress-responsive transcription factor networks [11].
On the CNS side, the marked flight-associated upregulation of mmu-miR-124-3p observed in our brain
cortical profiles is consistent with the broader literature documenting CNS adaptation under spaceflight
conditions. Roy-O’Reilly et al. reviewed evidence for alterations to the brain during spaceflight, including
neuroinflammatory and structural changes, and highlighted the potential relevance of such adaptations to crew
health in long-duration space exploration [12]. Similarly, Holley et al. characterized gene expression profiles
in the spaceflight mouse brain following a 35-day ISS mission and identified 54 differentially expressed
genes with significant pathway-level shifts in neuroinflammation, cellular stress, apoptosis, and metabolism
in flight versus ground control tissue [14]. Taken together, these cross-OSDR contextualizations suggest that
both the hepatic and CNS expression shifts identified in our candidate co-expression axis are biologically
plausible and directionally consistent with independent spaceflight transcriptomic evidence, strengthening
the credibility of the associations reported here despite the small-n constraint of the present cohort.

4.4 Implications for Multi-Organ Hypothesis Generation
If a spaceflight biological system exhibits candidate cross-tissue miRNA co-expression dependencies, de-
signing countermeasures targeting isolated organs may be insufficient. Monitoring central nervous system
markers without also tracking the underlying hepatic expression environment may represent an incomplete
observational approach, as the peripheral liver miRNA profile may co-vary with downstream CNS expression
states in directions that are not always intuitive.
Future biomedical hypotheses should enforce a multi-organ observational approach. By utilizing this
predictive computational framework, aerospace medical researchers can identify peripheral, accessible
tissue miRNA profiles (such as mmu-miR-17-5p) as candidate indicators of downstream central nervous
system miRNA expression trends. Importantly, the static co-expression associations identified here represent
candidate predictive correlations and should not be interpreted as established physiological mechanisms
without wet-lab validation.
4.5 Limitations and Future Work
While our regularized framework offers a novel lens on systemic spaceflight miRNA co-expression biology,
several distinct limitations must be explicitly acknowledged, including methodological constraints surfaced
by a reproducibility audit of the underlying codebase. First, the RRRM-1 cohort is small (n = 9), reflecting
the strict physical and logistical constraints of ISS payloads [4]. Replicating these candidate predictive
cross-tissue associations across larger, multi-modal human and animal datasets within the NASA OSDR will
be essential to determine whether the mmu-miR-17-5p liver-brain candidate axis generalizes across diverse
biological cohorts and longer spaceflight durations. Companion OSDR datasets from RR-1, RR-3, RR-6, and
RR-9 [6, 7] represent natural extensions for future validation, as do the human astronaut multi-omic profiles
assembled through the NASA GeneLab platform [4, 5].
Second, the experimental cohort comprises 3 spaceflight, 3 habitat control, and 3 vivarium control animals.
Accordingly, “flight” vs. “ground” comparisons reflect a three-group design with extremely limited per-
condition replication, and all flight-associated expression trends should be interpreted with appropriate
caution.
Third, the current pipeline is strictly correlational: it captures co-expression trends between peripheral
liver miRNA inputs and central brain miRNA targets within a static cross-sectional dataset, and the network’s
stochastic initialization means reported metrics should be interpreted as representative rather than fully
deterministic. Five repeated runs produced VMSE values ranging from approximately 1.011 to 1.206,
reflecting the sensitivity to initialization seed.
Fourth, global Z-score normalization is applied before the LOOCV split in the current implementation,
meaning held-out samples influence scaling statistics. This constitutes a mild preprocessing leakage; under
fully leakage-free fold-wise normalization, VMSE estimates were higher (approximately 1.28–1.71). Future
implementations should apply scaler fitting exclusively on training folds.
Fifth, SHAP feature attribution is computed on the final-fold model instance rather than aggregated across
all LOOCV fold models. Reported SHAP magnitudes are therefore single-model estimates and may vary
across repeated runs. The ranking of mmu-miR-17-5p as the dominant feature was consistent across runs,
but exact Shapley values should not be treated as stable numerical quantities. Future wet-lab validation—
specifically utilizing targeted miRNA knockdown models or localized hepatic antioxidant interventions in
simulated microgravity systems—is required to determine whether any causal or mechanistic relationship
underlies the identified co-expression patterns. Future work will also explore the integration of multi-tissue
variational autoencoders (β-V AEs) to explicitly separate task-specific environmental expression shifts from
baseline inter-subject physiological variance, alongside repeated-seed robustness analyses to quantify variance
attributable to stochastic initialization [17].

5. Conclusion
We present a constrained deep learning analysis of candidate cross-tissue miRNA co-expression associations
induced by the spaceflight environment. Through a regularized PyTorch Multi-Layer Perceptron framework
applied to a focused signature of 6 peripheral liver and 5 central brain miRNA features from female
BALB/cAnNTac mice (RRRM-1 / RR-8; GEO Accession: GSE294046) [3], we report empirical findings
suggesting that traditional single-tissue workflows may miss candidate system-wide multi-organ miRNA
co-expression patterns. Our computational framework mapped a candidate predictive cross-tissue miRNA
co-expression axis with an informative LOOCV validation mean squared error of 1.1062, revealing a complex,
divergent regulatory landscape in which spaceflight-associated hepatic miRNA shifts—anchored by mmu-
miR-17-5p—co-occur with a marked upregulation of central mmu-miR-124-3p and a heterogeneous, mixed
response across the let-7 family. Cross-referencing with companion spaceflight datasets available through
NASA OSDR [4, 6, 7] confirms that these hepatic and CNS expression patterns are broadly consistent with
convergent spaceflight phenotypes documented across independent Rodent Research payloads.
By establishing mmu-miR-17-5p as the primary candidate peripheral tracking anchor within this cor-
relational liver-brain axis, this study introduces an empirical pilot tool for mapping predictive miRNA
co-expression trends within high-dimensional, sample-sparse spaceflight data. Future aerospace biomedi-
cal research may benefit from moving beyond isolated organ tracking and idealized uniform-suppression
assumptions toward integrated, multi-tissue miRNA profiling paradigms that embrace the complexity of
divergent regulatory responses. Monitoring peripheral hepatic miRNA shifts as candidate indicators of central
nervous system expression states represents a promising hypothesis for future targeted investigation toward
dependable, multi-organ biomedical strategies to safeguard astronaut neurological health during long-duration
interplanetary missions.
